1

Where this sits

The composed-wordmark round ended with six things it had found and not fixed. This round closes four of them, and they turned out to have one thing in common that none of them had separately.

In each case something written down disagreed with the pictures. A measurement said a poster had no dot screen when it does. The character description asked for a head nobody has been drawing. Three briefs asked for a figure at one fifth of the frame and got one at nearly half. And a new check, built to replace a manual look, disagreed with a poster that had already shipped.

Three times the writing was wrong. Once the picture was. Telling those apart is the whole of the work below, and it is not something any of these files could do for themselves.

Three image generations, 225 credits. Everything else is deterministic.

3

The dots came from the drawing — and the meter still says smooth

The previous round tried three ways to paint a halftone onto the Senate Square poster and abandoned all of them, on a limit Marcus named: “It’s more meaningful if it is a certain element in the image. Not just applied to a certain hue with a certain strength.” A colour-plus-threshold mask has no notion of an element, so the screen had to come from the generator instead.

So the picture’s style block gained a section that names three things: the size of the dot, the flats it belongs in — night sky, shadowed hull, unlit stone — and the lit surfaces it must stay off. It worked.

And the measurement did not move enough to notice. The pipeline decides whether to add its own screen by measuring the source; above 0.035 it leaves the picture alone. Three separate generations came back at 0.0334–0.0349 — up 23% and consistently just short.

Humanoid worship0.044drawn by the generator
wild-cassette, the style reference0.071drawn by the generator
Senate Square, new0.033drawn by the generator — and unseen
Senate Square, previous0.028nowhere; it took a painted screen

The threshold cannot be reached by a correctly screened picture, and that is a fault in the threshold. The measurement averages fine detail across the whole frame, and it was calibrated against pictures that are busy everywhere. This screen is deliberately confined to the flats — and here those flats are a near-black night sky, where a dot has very little brightness to vary by. Doing the thing the rule asks for is what makes it invisible to the meter.

So the poster still tells the pipeline not to add a screen, and the reason written beside that instruction has changed: not we cannot place one, but the drawing already has one and the meter cannot see it. Fixing the meter means asking it about the flats rather than the frame, and that is left open rather than guessed at.

5

The 64-pixel check becomes a gate

Whether the wordmark survives being shrunk to a phone-sized thumbnail had been checked by whoever remembered to look. It had already changed two real decisions that way — a thin ring that reads on a poster and vanishes at 64 pixels is why the rings on these posters are as thick as they are.

The existing gate cannot see this. It measures the mark against the artwork at poster size, and a stroke can pass that comfortably and still dissolve when the picture is made twenty times smaller.

The new check asks a different question: does the word still have holes in it? Letters are legible because of the gaps — inside the P, inside the O, between the letters. When a thin stroke is resampled down, the ink and the gaps average into one grey and the word becomes a bar. That is measurable directly, by comparing what is left of the ink against what is left of its own counters.

Judged first, fitted second, the rule rediscovers a boundary nobody told it: it fails the 6- and 9-pixel rings and passes the 22- and 30-pixel ones. The 9-pixel ring is the exact width the previous round tried and abandoned by hand.

ring weightmeasuredthe eye
6 px1.99fails
9 px2.05fails
14 px2.14marginal
22 px2.18reads
30 px2.11reads

And it says thicker is not safer. The 30-pixel ring scores below the 22-pixel one, because a very heavy stroke starts closing the counters it exists to leave open. That is the measure describing something real rather than just counting ink.

The half that was thrown away

The check was built with two numbers. The second compared the mark to the artwork around it, and on the first corpus it looked like the stronger of the two by a factor of ten — a comfortable margin against a razor-thin one.

Its margin collapsed to almost nothing the moment a second picture was added to the corpus. That is not bad luck; it is the thing the first version had already written down as the reason to distrust it. A number that compares the mark to the artwork is a property of the artwork, and a corpus with one picture in it cannot test that. It is still calculated and printed. It decides nothing.

one groundthree grounds
structure — ink against its own countersgap 0.06gap 0.06held. This is the gate
ground — ink against the artworkgap 0.65gap 0.04reported only

An earlier version of the check failed differently and worse: it counted only pixels that were almost entirely ink, and at 64 pixels no ring has any, so it scored a mark that reads and a mark that does not identically at zero. The calibration caught that before the check was wired to anything.

6

What it found on work that had already shipped

The worship poster’s wordmark measures 1.20 against a threshold of 2.08 — the worst reading anywhere in the corpus, and not a borderline call. At the larger size it is fine.

The previous round had already seen this. It recorded that poster’s colour as the weakest of six at 64 px, and shipped it anyway. So the check’s first act was to fail a picture that is already out, for a reason a person had written down and moved past.

Marcus’s call: that picture is not a cover. It holds as a poster and at the middle size, and nothing has ever committed it to a thumbnail. The poster now says so in a single word that the build prints loudly on every run — because the round before last already learned what happens to an escape clause nobody can see.

Three ways out were considered and rejected before that one. A thicker stroke does not help, for the reason §5 measured. Filling the letterforms fixes the number and abandons the one thing that treatment exists to do, which is let the sky read through the word. A darker face would separate from the beam, and the palette has no dark neutral that is not the Ink.

7

The canon was wrong about the back of his head

The written character description — the paragraph pasted verbatim into every prompt — asks for a head with “nothing above or behind those arches: no second tier, no rear plate and no cranium.” Every poster has one.

A note written the day before had described this as no lower jaw at all and a projecting muzzle, across all three pictures. Looking at them at full size, that is wrong on both counts. The jaw is there and it is the narrow one the character round chose; the cheek arches and the heart-shaped nose are exactly as written. One clause diverged, not the head.

That is why the text was wrong. It was written to control a face-on character sheet, where you cannot see the back of a skull. What it then claimed about the back was never tested by the picture it was describing — and every poster shows him from an angle, where there is a compact rounded rear cranium.

Marcus has twice looked at that head and said it is right. So the fix is the narrowest one: the front silhouette stays specified exactly as before, and the back stops being forbidden, with a bound on it — never taller than the brow, never a high domed forehead. Nothing was regenerated, because fourteen posters were already drawing this.

The alternative is what makes the choice easy: keeping the sentence would have meant regenerating fourteen approved pictures to remove a feature everyone likes.

8

One fifth was never once one fifth

Two of the three briefs ask for Böbbis at “about one fifth of the image height”. Measured crown to boots:

posterthe briefmeasuredwhat he actually is
Kauppatoriabout one fifth43%the foreground subject
Senate Squareabout one fifth48%+the foreground subject — his legs run off the bottom edge, so his true height is nearer 56%
Humanoid worshipno fraction given36%the subject, by design

This is not the rule quietly changing. The rule already allows two ways to use him: small, placed on a real surface inside the scene, or front and centre as the subject. Worship is honestly the second and says so. The other two were briefed as the first and arrived as the second — a perfectly legal option that nobody chose.

Marcus took update the rule over enforce the fraction, and there is a reason beyond convenience. The character round already established that this generator does not do quantities, it does shapes. A fraction is a dial it has to set, exactly like an earlier request for just the hinge and the back teeth that came back with a full row. The clauses that hold are the ones naming a thing that can be drawn or not drawn.

So a poster now names its device rather than a number. Never large still binds, and only ever bound the small in-scene case.

The three existing prompts were not edited. A poster file carries the verbatim text that produced its picture; rewriting it to match a new rule would make the file lie about its own artwork, which is the exact failure that discipline exists to prevent. The stale sentence stays there as history.

9

What this concluded

  • A measurement can be defeated by the rule it is enforcing. The halftone meter cannot see a screen that is correctly confined to the flats, because it averages the whole frame. Doing the right thing is what made it invisible.
  • Judge first, measure second — and it earned its keep twice here. It killed the first version of the new check outright, and it caught the second one’s stronger- looking number being an artefact of a corpus with one picture in it.
  • A wide margin on a narrow corpus is not a wide margin. It just has not been tested yet.
  • Three of four disagreements were the writing’s fault, not the artwork’s. Files do not notice that they have gone stale, and no gate reads.
  • The one that was the artwork’s fault had already been seen and overridden. Automating a check does not find new problems so much as stop old ones being waved through.

Method, and what this cannot show

  • The new check’s corpus was judged by Claude, not by a person — three negatives across sixteen cells. The previous round calls four negatives a thin basis; this is thinner. It reproduces one boundary that was known independently and one failure that had been noticed and overridden, which is the strongest thing that can be said for it and is not the same as proven.
  • The same caveat still sits on the older gate, whose fifty test images were also judged by Claude. Both sets of sheets are on disk and re-judging them by eye is twenty minutes of work that nobody has done.
  • That older gate still slices vertically only. A mark that dies over one bright object mid-line would pass it. Unchanged this round.
  • The halftone meter is diagnosed, not fixed. §3.
  • The new Senate Square picture has not been seen by the band, and it replaces one that they signed off.
  • Nothing here was seen on paper. Screen only, and no check of how any of it survives a streaming service’s cover-art compression — which is notable given that this round is entirely about what happens to a mark when it gets small.
  • All three posters now use the same device for the character, so the variety that rule was protecting has quietly gone. A fourth poster placing him small on a real surface would test whether that option still works; nothing has tested it since wave three.
  • Three generations, 225 credits. Everything else is deterministic and re-runnable from the poster files.
