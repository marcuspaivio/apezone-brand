The band has now seen this work. Reported 2026‑08‑30: every member read the shared material and looked at the artwork. They like the wordmark, the skull, the year rings and the timeline, and they love the illustrated generated work.

That is a response to a direction, not a signature on each item. Individual artefacts, whether Böbbis becomes a standing per-release character, whether the skull is ever publicly named, and printing or selling anything are all still open. Where a direction was chosen inside a round, that was Marcus’s call.

Start with the plain-language overview if you haven’t already.

The question

What happens above the brow ridge? The detailed skull’s cranium is near-black on a near-black ground, so it disappears — and the only part of it that reads is its two upper corners, which look like horns. Everything below the brow was settled and out of scope.

The answer

Nothing. The cranium comes off, and the brow’s own top contour is drawn as the edge where the drawing stops. The mark becomes the front of the skull: no jaw, no cranium. A fragment, twice, for the same reason.

Marcus’s call, made looking at the comparison sheet, and the reasoning he gave is the strongest thing in the round: the ape has no jaw anyway, so it makes sense only the front is visible. Seven crowns were drawn to answer this question and none of them beat deleting the question.

THE PROBLEM

It only shows on a bright ground

On the near-black ground the identity uses by default, the top of the head is simply not there — and nobody notices, because there is nothing to notice. Put the same drawing on a busy or bright background and the cranium comes back, but only in the two places where its outline is thick enough to catch: the shoulders. Those read as horns.

What had already been tried, and why it failed

Cropping the cranium off removes the horns and immediately reads as a cropped photograph, because the brow ridges are left with flat straight tops. Trimming the cut to follow the contour gives the same result. The lesson from that attempt, and the rule this round worked to: a new outline cannot be made by deleting or recolouring pixels. It has to be drawn.

THE MEASUREMENT

The question had a false premise

Before drawing anything, the mark was measured. The result reframed the whole round.

Above the brow, the drawing contains no light tone at all

Measured on the mark as built: above the brow ridges, it holds zero bone-coloured pixels and 67,708 near-black ones. The cranium and the ridge along the top of it — the one feature that makes the mark read as an ape rather than a generic skull — are both drawn as dark linework floating in a hollow the drawing never closes.

So on the ground the brand actually uses, they are not faint. They are absent, and have been all along. The question was never should the cranium go. It went years ago. What is left is an invisible shape still doing two jobs, both bad.

The pattern in that last column is the round’s second finding

Five crowns drawn from scratch, five different curves, and four of them read as headgear — a hood, a cowl, a brim, a helmet spike. That is not four failures of draughtsmanship.

The shape below the brow is so emphatically a face that anything smooth placed above it reads as something worn rather than as bone. It is the same mechanism that made the earlier crop read as a cropped photograph: the drawing has already declared where it ends. Which is a fairly direct argument for letting it end there.

HORNS

Tested properly, because it was a repositioning

Marcus raised this and the argument for it was sound: the corners already read as horns by accident, and leaning into what a shape is doing anyway usually beats fighting it. So it was given a real test rather than a token one — but as a change of proposition, not as a tidy-up. Apes do not have horns; the mark would stop being a specimen and become a creature.

Verdict, stated plainly as the brief asked for: horns are a one-off poster or merch device, not the identity. The loud register already exists for exactly that — one shirt, one poster, knowingly — and that is where this belongs.

A CROWN OR A HELMET

Raised mid-round, answered from the evidence

Marcus also floated putting an actual object on the head — a crown, a helmet. It was not drawn as a variant, because it is a different question (what sits on the head, rather than where the head ends) and the round’s own question had just been answered. What the seven drawings already say about it:

  • The mark keeps producing this read by accident. Four of the five drawn crowns landed on headgear without being asked to. Adding a deliberate helmet doubles down on a read the design has been trying to shed.
  • It is the costume failure by name. The platform names a young band’s brand worn by men in their forties and fifties as the single most damaging thing this relaunch could do. A crown on an ape skull also walks straight into the comedy-monkey trap — king of the apes — which the same document calls the easiest trap here, and the one the band’s name walks into.
  • It reduces exactly like the horns do, and for the same reason: a projecting object above the brow is the first thing to die, and it takes the outline with it.

Same slot as the horns: a per-release or merch device, not the identity. A per-release artwork programme already exists in the system, and an object-on-the-head fits that shape far better than it fits a logo.

THE ANSWER

One curve, and a deletion

The chosen version is not a crop. A crop was tried and failed. This deletes the cranium and draws the contour that closes the brow, so the mark reads as a plate that stops rather than a drawing that ran out.

The drawn edge sits entirely above the bone, so on the dark ground it changes nothing at all and the version Marcus picked is preserved exactly. It is doing its work on paper and on busy backgrounds — and there it is the whole job: delete the cranium without it and the ridges bleed straight into the sheet.

WHAT THIS CHANGES

Less than it sounds

The big skull Cranium deleted, brow contour drawn. This is the drawing that goes on posters, shirts and sleeves, and the one the background mockups use.
The small skull — unchanged Checked, not assumed. The profile-picture version is a different drawing: its cranium is a solid light mass and its ridge reads clearly at 32 pixels. It has none of this problem, and its specification stands.
The ridge is not lost from the identity It lives where it always did — in the small skull at profile-picture sizes, and in the detailed large-format drawing. What was deleted is one drawing’s phantom cranium, which was carrying nothing.
Still open — how the big skull gets depth Untouched by this round and deliberately so. The earlier attempt at depth and material collapsed into tool-building; that question is still waiting.
The caveat that should not be buried

This round worked on the drawing that appears in the background mockups — and the review had already retired that particular drawing in favour of a different, more detailed one for large-format use. Both of the drawings actually specified were then checked, and neither has this problem: in both of them the top of the skull is a solid shape rather than a hollow outline.

So the fault was specific to one retired drawing. The decision is not. “Only the front of the skull is visible” is a call about what shape the mark is, and it should carry over to whichever drawing ends up being the final one — but applied there it is a deliberate removal of something that currently works, not a repair. That is a bigger call than this round needed, and it should be made with that in view.

If the ridge has to appear on this drawing too

There is a one-line fallback, no redraw: give the cranium’s outline the light tone instead of the dark one — light on the dark ground, dark on paper, which is how everything else in the identity already flips. It is the only version tested that shows the ridge on a dark ground at all, and it would be the first time it ever has. The cost is that the cranium reads as a hoop rather than a solid form, and the mark stays a third smaller in any square space.

HONEST LIMITS

What this round cannot tell you

  • None of this is artwork. The drawn contour is a uniform-width stroke; the rest of the drawing has weight that varies. A real redraw would taper it. These images exist to settle a shape.
  • The weakest claim in the round is the one about apes. Without the ridge, this reads as a heavy fanged primate skull — and honestly, someone with no context could land on a large predator rather than a gorilla. That was judged by looking at it small, by the person who drew it, which is the least reliable possible reviewer. It is worth a second pair of eyes.
  • Nothing seen on paper or on a real phone. Every size claim is a render at nominal pixel size — a good proxy, not the thing.
  • The drawing’s known problems are untouched — the barbed flicks and angled eye sockets flagged in the review are still there, and they are part of why the review retired this drawing. Out of scope here, still owed a pass.
  • No image generation was used. Nothing was generated; the budget was 6–10 and 0 were spent. Everything is the band’s own drawing plus curves placed by hand.
