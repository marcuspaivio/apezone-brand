The question

Can one skull drawing work at every size, from a poster down to the little icon in a browser tab?

The word APEZONE was finished on 2 August. The skull was not. The plan had always been that the badge, the profile picture and the tab icon would all be the same drawing with detail taken out — not a second, simpler skull drawn separately. Two skulls that look like cousins is how an identity goes soft.

The answer

No — and the files ship anyway, as two drawings instead of one. Above 48 pixels the identity uses the detailed skull. Below it, a simpler drawing from an earlier round. They are clearly related, and they are not the same object.

It was built the intended way first, and Marcus rejected the small end on sight. He was right, and the reason it failed is the most useful thing on this page: whether a drawing can be made small is decided when it is drawn, not when it is shrunk.

NOT ARTWORK

What is finished here and what is not

Read this before looking at anything below
  • The skull is finished artwork. Unlike the earlier rounds, this is not a concept or a generated image — it is a real drawing in a real vector file, and it is what the files contain. It can be judged as final.
  • But its origins are messier than the word’s. The outline was rebuilt from an image a machine generated back in round B, then cleaned up, straightened and re-drawn. The features inside it come from Juha Syrjänen’s 2012 drawing or were drawn fresh. This is a weaker claim to originality than the wordmark has, and a proper originality check has still not been done.
  • Below 48 pixels it is a different drawing — round B’s simpler skull. Two related drawings rather than one. That was not the plan and it is not ideal; the section What actually happened below explains why it is still the right call. Both are front-of-the-face only, with no top of the head.
  • The small drawing is a traced machine generation too, from round B. It is the one the specification always named for small sizes, and it survived a whole round that tried to beat it — but it is not hand artwork either.
  • Nothing here has been printed, or seen in a real browser tab. Every picture on this page is an exact-pixel render.
THE CROWN

Tested once more, and dropped once more

Two earlier rounds had settled that this skull is the front of the face only — no jaw, and no top of the head. Marcus asked for the top of the head to be looked at one last time, pointing at a reference with a rounded braincase.

It was tested properly, and the evidence at small sizes actually supported adding one. That is worth saying plainly, because the decision went the other way.

Why the small-size evidence did not win

The grid proves that adding mass above the eyes rescues the tiny sizes. It does not prove that a skull cap is the right way to add it — any lump would have scored the same, and this lump reads as a bread roll.

An earlier round had already named the mechanism and it caught this attempt too: the shape below the brow is so strongly a face that anything smooth and continuous placed above it reads as something worn rather than as bone. The first pass looked like a helmet; rounding it off only changed which hat it was.

Marcus’s call: no crown, no top of the head. The two earlier rounds stand — six attempts at headgear, six rejections. The versions that were built are kept in the working folder as the record of what was tried.

THE PROBLEM

A drawing that will not shrink

With the shape settled, making the small versions should have been the easy part. It was not, and the reason is a genuinely odd property of this particular drawing.

The eye sockets are not holes

In most drawings the eye sockets are shapes — two dark almonds you could point at and delete. In this one they are the gaps left between strokes. There is no “socket” object; there is a mesh of light lines, and the eyes are what you see between them.

That means the obvious way to simplify a drawing — throw away the finest lines and keep the big ones — deletes every feature at once. It was tried: cutting the drawing from 17 pieces to 11 to 7 to 5 changed nothing visible at all, and at 3 pieces the skull became a blank shield with two fangs. There is no useful middle.

You can see the consequence directly. On the left of each pair below is the finished drawing simply shrunk; on the right is what this round built instead.

THE METHOD

Fill it in solid, then name the parts

The fix is to work with the gaps rather than the lines. Three steps, all done by the same script, all starting from the one finished drawing:

  1. Fill the skull in solid. Flood the outline so it becomes one solid shape instead of a mesh of separate pieces. This is the whole trick — at small sizes what survives is mass, and the solid shape is the mass.
  2. Work out what the gaps are. Compare the solid shape with the drawing and what is left over is every gap: the eyes, the nose, the teeth, the cheeks — and a lot of thin channels between strokes that are not features at all.
  3. Give the real ones names, and close them in a fixed order as the picture gets smaller.
Why the parts had to be named rather than ranked

The obvious shortcut is to keep the biggest gaps and close the smallest. It produces the wrong animal. Measured, the two fattest gaps in the drawing are not the eyes — they are two notches at the top corners of the head. Rank by size and the ladder throws away the eyes and keeps the notches, and the result reads as a creature with ears.

So the order is set by hand and written down: the eyes and the fangs are the last two things to go, because an eyeless skull is not a skull. Anatomy has to be named or it gets optimised away.

And one more correction, from looking at it

Some gaps are too thin to see at a small size even when they are kept, so they get widened. The first attempt widened them in every direction — and the two eye sockets grew into each other and merged into a single bar across the face. A shield with a stripe on it is exactly the “blob” the 16-pixel test is supposed to catch.

The fix: an eye socket is not too narrow, it is too shallow. Widen each gap only across its thin direction — eyes and teeth get taller, fangs and nose get wider — and nothing runs into its neighbour.

What this got right, and it is worth keeping

Because every version is the same solid shape with different bits closed, the outline is identical at all of them — not similar, identical. As a piece of machinery it does exactly what was asked. It is the drawing that will not go small, and no amount of machinery fixes that.

WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED

The small end was built, shipped, and sent back

All of the above was finished and in the file set. Marcus looked at it and said: “The ramp is bad. The ones under 48px… I would actually use the incumbent skull shape instead.”

The incumbent is round B’s skull — the simpler one, drawn back in the first exploration, which the specification had named for small sizes all along and which an entire later round (round D) failed to beat with twelve generated challengers. Here it is against what had just been built.

Why it lost — and this is the useful part of the whole page

The two drawings fail differently for one reason. In the detailed skull the eye sockets are gaps between strokes. In round B’s skull they are holes — closed shapes with their own edges.

Closing a gap preserves where the feature was. It does not preserve that you can see it. A hole survives being made small because it is a shape; a gap just closes. Whether a drawing can be made small is decided when it is drawn, not when it is shrunk — and round B built its skull out of closed shapes deliberately, which is why a drawing from the very first exploration is still the one that works.

The cost, stated plainly

The identity is now two skull drawings, changing over at 48 pixels. Both are the front of the face with no top of the head, so they are close relatives — but they are not one object seen near and far.

That is exactly the problem this task was created to fix, back again with the sizes swapped round. It is a real regression and it is recorded as one. The alternative was a small mark that does not read, which is worse: an icon nobody can make out is not a cheaper version of an icon, it is a broken one.

One thing was got wrong here and then fixed. Round B’s drawing has a ridge on top of the skull, and the first version of this shipped with it — argued for on the grounds that it is a solid shape rather than the hollow outline earlier rounds deleted, so it adds useful mass at tiny sizes. Marcus spotted it the next morning: hadn’t the top of the head already been settled, three times? It had. Looking again, the evidence didn’t even support keeping it — without the ridge is the better mark at 48, 32 and 24, and only marginally worse at 16. It is off — the right-hand column above is what ships, the middle one is kept for comparison.

LIGHT AND DARK

One drawing, and which half of it is inked

A late change, and a good one. Looking at this very report on a phone, Marcus said he would rather have the version where the pale shapes give the mass and the eye sockets stay dark — and then, on the light version, that the existing one is right there, “the same but with the dark outlines”.

Both together turn out to be a rule rather than two preferences. The skull is two-tone, and whichever tone matches the background disappears. Nothing is redrawn: the two are exact opposites of each other, and painting both over the same square fills it completely.

Why this is tidier than it sounds

An earlier note in the working record said “the mark is the pale shapes; the outline is optional”. This finishes that thought: the outline is the mark on paper, and the fill is the mark on dark. They were always the same file, seen two ways.

One quirk worth knowing, because it is the bit that makes it work: the brow is not joined to the cheeks, so when the drawing is filled in solid the background runs in through those gaps and the eye sockets are left outside the filled area. That is exactly why they stay dark rather than filling in.

This does not change the small end. The dark version was run down the ramp too and it fails the same way the light one does — below 48 pixels it is still round B’s drawing, in one geometry, because that one reads correctly either way up.

THE FILES

What can actually be used now

Everything is generated by script from the two drawings, so if either is ever corrected the whole set is rebuilt by running four commands — there is no hand-exported file anywhere that could quietly fall out of date.

Three rules that come with them
  • Use the file made for the size you are placing. Never shrink a big one. The 512-pixel badge scaled down to 16 is grey mush; the 16-pixel file is not, because it is a different drawing.
  • Below 96 pixels the skull always sits on a dark tile, browser-tab icon included. Every version of this mark, across four rounds of work, falls apart as dark-on- light below about 32 pixels. On paper it prints as ordinary dark-on-white artwork, because print is never small.
  • On a mid-grey or busy background it needs an outline, added as a separate layer for that one surface. It is not part of the mark and it is not in these files.
One small thing decided along the way

All three sizes inside favicon.ico are the small drawing, including the 48 — even though the badge at 48 is the big one. A single icon file is one file that the operating system picks a size out of depending on where it lands: tab strip, bookmark bar, desktop shortcut. If the 48 inside it were the other drawing, the same file would show two different skulls depending on context. Inside one file, consistency wins.

METHOD AND LIMITS

What this page cannot tell you

  • No generations were used. Nothing on this page was produced by asking a machine to draw a skull. The work is arithmetic on an existing drawing: filling, measuring, closing and widening, all reproducible by re-running one script.
  • Nothing has been printed or seen on a real phone, and the 16-pixel icon has not been seen in a real browser tab. Every claim about size on this page is an exact-pixel render, which is a good proxy and not the thing itself.
  • The small versions were judged by one pair of eyes, on the sheets shown here. Nobody who had not been staring at the drawing for hours has looked at the 16-pixel version and said what they see. That is exactly the check the browser-tab test is for.
  • Where the outline came from is a weaker story than the word’s. It descends from an image generated in an earlier round, rebuilt and corrected rather than traced. The project’s own rule against tracing generated images is met in substance, but this is not the same as “it comes from the band’s own artwork”, and it should not be described that way.
  • The alternative that would actually fix it was not pursued: redrawing the big skull with its eyes and nose as proper closed shapes, so one drawing could serve every size. It would have meant throwing away the drawing that had just been approved, on the same day. It remains the real fix and it is written down as such.
  • Where the line sits is a judgement, not a measurement. Marcus said “under 48” and that is where it went. Looking at the sheet, round B’s skull is arguably the better mark at 48 as well — that was not changed, because the instruction was specific and the big drawing does hold there.
