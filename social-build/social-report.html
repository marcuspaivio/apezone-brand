Two jobs, one sitting

The band reassembled in August 2026 and starts rehearsing on the 7th, and Marcus was explicit that things go out during that season rather than after it: “we might release to some on the way, some teasers etc.” Two things had to be true before the first teaser. The accounts have to carry the right artwork, and somebody has to have decided what a teaser looks like. Splitting them would have meant the first clip shipping before the rules existed.

The first half is production and invents nothing: every file is a mark that already existed, rendered at a number that came off a platform’s own help page. The second half is a written section in art-direction.md, which until this morning said outright that it did not cover video.

Nothing here has been uploaded, and nothing has been posted. That is tasks 5.1 and 5.2 and it needs Marcus. This produced the files and the rules.

There is no photograph of the band anywhere in it. Task 3.5 is deferred until the five have played together, and no generated substitute exists or may be made.

1

Every number, and where it came from

The single most likely way to get this task wrong is to take the pixel dimensions from memory, or from a design blog. Platform specs move, and a wrong number ships a blurry avatar to eight places at once. So every number below was read off the platform’s own current help documentation on 3 August 2026, and the URL and the date sit on the row in assets/social/SPECS.md.

Half the platforms refuse an ordinary fetch, so this was done in a real browser. It took most of an hour and it found four things that no summary would have.

The spec sheet keeps three words apart and never mixes them: documented means the platform states the number; derived means we computed it from one the platform states, and the arithmetic is shown; ours means the platform publishes nothing and we chose. An undocumented number is not a wrong number, but it must never wear the platform’s authority.

Four things that only turn up if you actually read the page

  • Instagram publishes no profile-picture size. None. Its own article on adding and changing your profile picture gives no pixel dimension, no file cap and no format list, and no other article does either. So the Instagram avatar here is ours, and labelled that way — 1024², chosen because an over-large square can only ever be scaled down by the platform.
  • Facebook contradicts itself three ways in four lines. The cover photo “left aligns with a full bleed and a 16:9 aspect ratio” on a computer, 2.4:1 on a mobile device, and loads fastest at 851 × 315, which is 2.70:1. No two agree. The cover is built at the recommended size with everything that matters inside the intersection of all three under a centre crop — 561 × 315, or 66% of the width. That region is ours; Facebook publishes no safe area.
  • YouTube gives a render size, not an upload size. “Image that renders at 98 × 98 px” and “should not exceed 15 MB” is the whole of the profile-picture spec. 800² is our choice.
  • Two platforms forbid text in an artist image. Spotify: “Don’t include text, advertising, or busy backgrounds.” Apple Music: “No marketing copy, third-party logos of any kind, or typography.” Both explicitly allow a band logo. So the Spotify header carries the skull, not the wordmark — a constraint that came from the platform rather than from us, and the design obeys it rather than arguing.
2

The set

Eight platforms, twenty-three files. Every avatar is the skull on its Ink tile — the tier system’s rule, unchanged. Every header is the wordmark and the pulse rule on an Ink field, except Spotify’s, which is the skull because Spotify forbids text.

Six rules, none of them new

Every one is somebody else’s decision, applied. That is the whole ambition of this half: production, not design.

  1. No header carries anything that has to be read. Gate 5 found this for the share image and it generalises to all eight: the platform picks the display size and a baked PNG cannot respond, so a floor that a live web page would honour by dropping an element is unenforceable here. Spotify and SoundCloud reach the same place from their own rules.
  2. Everything is centred. Not taste. Facebook covers the left of the cover with the avatar, X overlays bottom left, YouTube’s safe area is a centred letterbox, and Facebook’s three aspect ratios only share a region taken centre-out. Centring is the one placement that satisfies all of them at once.
  3. The lockup is at least 62% of the pulse rule’s width on every header. Gate 5 measured that below 60% the mark reads as a caption to its own furniture. Here it holds by construction: the pulse is sized from the mark rather than from the canvas.
  4. Bone on Ink, one warm element — the pulse rule’s hollow 2027 tick, on every surface.
  5. One cap height of clear space, asserted at build time against the safe box rather than the canvas, because the canvas edge is not what crops.
  6. File sizes are asserted against each platform’s published cap. Nothing is close — the largest file is 54 KB against a 20 MB ceiling, because flat vector on a flat ground compresses to almost nothing. The check exists for the day somebody puts a photograph behind the wordmark.

The first version of this build did not look like a set. The pulse rule was sized as a share of each canvas and then the mark had to fit in what was left; on X and on YouTube — the two shortest safe areas — it did not, so those two came out as a bare wordmark on a field while the other four carried the strip. Deriving the strip from the mark instead of from the canvas fixed both at once.

3

The crop proof

The commonest way this task fails is a header that reads perfectly on a desktop and loses the mark behind an avatar bubble on a phone. So every header is shown with its crop region drawn on it: inside the amber box is what the platform guarantees, and the washed-out area outside it is what may be gone.

Three of the eight publish no safe area at all. For those, the box is a centred 2.4:1 window — Facebook’s documented mobile cover aspect, the widest mobile container documented anywhere in the set, borrowed from a neighbour rather than invented. It is labelled ours on the sheet and in the spec file. The defence that actually matters is not the box: it is that nothing on any of these headers has to be read, so wherever the crop lands, it costs nothing.

The first crop proof proved nothing, and shipped that way for one build. The at-risk area was dimmed with Ink — over an Ink ground. It changed nothing at all. On this palette the only direction available is lighter, so the wash is Steel.

4

The avatar nearly broke a settled rule. It doesn’t

The identity splits at 48 pixels. Above it, the detailed front-of-skull; below it, round B’s simpler drawing — because a socket that began as a gap between strokes cannot be made small, while a socket that is a hole with its own edges survives anything. Marcus settled that on 2 August, and the usage sheet states the rule as use the file cut for the size you are placing.

Every avatar here is placed at 320 pixels or larger, so the rule says the detailed skull and that is what ships. But there is a real question sitting underneath it, and the measurement behind it is worth keeping.

A social avatar is not placed at one size. One uploaded file is met at whatever size the platform picks — Facebook says in its own words that a single 320-pixel upload renders at 176, 196 and 36 pixels. So the split cannot be honoured, and one drawing has to cover the whole range. Which one?

Scaled down, the detailed skull’s eye sockets are gone by 48 px and the brow reads as a moustache; at 32 it is not a skull at all. The simpler drawing holds at both, and at 128 and above it is perfectly good — plainer, not worse.

That was nearly shipped as a change, and it should not have been. The argument only works if the avatar really is met at 32 pixels — and that is the one part that was never verified. The only documented small render anywhere in this set is Facebook’s 36 pixels on feature phones. X’s timeline row and Instagram’s feed are published by nobody, and asserting them from memory is precisely the failure this whole task was written to avoid.

On the sizes that are documented — Facebook’s 176 and 196, YouTube’s 98 — the detailed skull is right, and the settled rule already says so. Evidence kept, decision left. Switching the whole set is one constant in the build if Marcus wants it.

5

A defect in something already shipped

Facebook and SoundCloud both state that the avatar is masked to a circle; X, Instagram, YouTube, Spotify and Apple Music do it without saying so. A circle inscribed in a square is inside that square everywhere except the corners — and the small skull’s widest ink, the outer tips of the brow, sits exactly there.

The shipped glyph tiles inset the mark to 86% of the tile, a number composed for a square. Measured rather than guessed, by brand/social-build/circle_fit.py:

The detailed skull (f8) — what these avatars use 0.00%0.863
The small skull (r4b)1.80% — 1508 of 83568 pixels0.817

The avatars here are fine — the detailed skull clears the mask with nothing to spare but nothing lost, so they keep the shipped 86% and a social avatar is the same object as a favicon at a different size. That is also a second, quieter argument for the drawing choice in §4.

But the same measurement, run over the files already delivered in assets/logos/, says apezone-glyph-circle-16.png, -24 and -32 lose 1.7–2.1% of their ink. Those are round B’s drawing — the ones the split hands the circle to, and the ones whose brow tips sit in the corners.

1.8% sounds like nothing, and the percentage is the wrong way to see it. At 32 px it is about six pixels — but they are the outer tips of the brow, which is the widest and most characteristic part of the silhouette. The loss does not read as a few missing pixels. It reads as the mark having been filed flat across the top.

That is a bug in a shipped asset, and it belongs to task 2.2, not to this one. It is reported rather than quietly rebuilt: the fix is to give build_assets.py’s png_tile(circle=True) a tighter inset than its square sibling, which regenerates the whole logo set — a change to a signed-off deliverable, and Marcus’s to make.

6

Teasers are in colour — so do we grade them?

The second half. platform.md names video as a place the brand lives and puts the band’s humour there; art-direction.md said in plain words that it did not cover video, on the grounds that it was a later problem. Rehearsal teasers made it today’s.

This section first recommended black and white, and Marcus overruled it the same day. Teasers are in colour. The photographs stay monochrome; the videos do not follow them. The measurement below is kept rather than deleted, because it still says something and because the argument it retires is the one somebody will otherwise make again.

The usual case for black and white is that rehearsal rooms are lit in ugly colours and black and white forgives that. The art direction’s strongest finding says it does not — a frame shot under saturated coloured stage light does not become restrained in monochrome, it becomes fog. But that was measured on a stage, and a rehearsal room is an ordinary room with ordinary bulbs. So the same metric was run over the band’s own archive, split by the light each frame was shot under:

An ordinary room comes out level with a properly lit stage. That was the case for monochrome and it is now moot — but two things survive it. A practice room is a photographable space rather than a flat one. And the coloured-bulb instruction stands, now for a blunter reason: in black and white a red-washed frame was unusable fog; in colour it is simply red all over, which is worse for a brand whose one rule about red is that there isn’t any.

So: grade, or leave as is?

Neither. Don’t grade — control the camera instead.

Don’t grade, for three reasons. There is no specification for what a colour Apezone picture looks like — none has ever been written — so grading toward one means inventing it, which is design and not this job. The tooling is not there and the grade would not get run, and a rule nobody follows is worse than no rule. And the register already argues for it: arrange the people, do not light them, and do not fix them afterwards.

But “leave them as is” is a trap, and this is the useful half of the answer. A phone does not give you ungraded video. It gives you its grade: saturation lifted, contrast pushed, sharpening, HDR tone-mapping, and an auto white balance that drifts between shots and inside them. The off-limits list already names the default look of a phone’s auto-enhance. Shipping the camera’s default is not neutrality — it is shipping the one look the brand explicitly forbids.

So the work moves from the edit to the moment of capture. Three controls, all free:

  1. Lock the white balance — the most valuable one by a distance. Auto WB drifts, so five clips from one rehearsal come back five different colours. Lock it once per room, and set it neutral rather than “as shot”: a tungsten practice room is orange, and neutral pulls it toward the brand’s cold register without anybody grading anything.
  2. Turn the phone’s enhancement off — HDR styling, “vivid” profiles, sharpening, skin smoothing. Flattest standard profile. Not log, which is unwatchable until graded and defeats the whole answer.
  3. Lock exposure once framed, so the picture does not pump when somebody walks past a window. Do not crush the blacks or blow the highlights — the platform re-encodes after you and takes another bite out of both.

Then afterwards, one correction that is not a grade: if the white balance drifted, fix the white balance. Nothing else. No LUT, no filter, no cinematic preset, no teal-and-orange. And no grain — grain goes on last at final output size, and on video there is no final output size, because the platform re-encodes after you and every codec treats grain as noise to smear away.

The real cost of choosing colour — and the scope it needed, now settled

Black and white made no red anywhere impossible to break. Colour removes that, and a rehearsal room contains a red amp jewel, a red cable, somebody’s red shirt. The rule cannot be obeyed literally by a camera pointed at a real room. So either the footage is monochrome after all, or the rule gets a scope:

Rule 5 governs the colour the brand chooses, not the colour the world is.

Forbidden: choosing red — dressing a shot with it, a red light, a red backdrop; grading toward red; and a red field, a wash that makes the whole frame red, which is the 2011 badge in motion. Not forbidden: a power LED, a cable, a shirt. Those are the room. The test carries over from the photography rules verbatim — could you point at the lamp?

And where the brand’s own colour appears on the clip — the end card, any type, any rule or scrim over the footage — rule 5 stays absolute. That is the clean line, and it is the same shape as §7’s answer about the jokes: the footage is a record, the graphics on it are the brand.

Adopted 2026-08-04

This was the only place the video section amended a settled document rather than applying one, and Marcus settled it: “I agree on the proposal. Makes sense. It will be almost impossible otherwise.” The scope now lives in colour.md rule 5 itself, in art-direction.md §9.4, and on the off-limits list, which is where a reader would otherwise hit the unscoped version first.

7

Where the humour lives — a real contradiction, resolved

The off-limits list bans visual gags outright: no monkey comedy, no banana, no gorilla suit, no ape hands, nothing that winks. The brand platform says releases and videos are the humour’s main home. Both are Marcus’s, both from the same week, and a rehearsal teaser is precisely where they collide.

They are not in conflict, and the obvious reading — the frame is serious, the content is funny — is not quite right either. The distinction that holds was already sitting inside the off-limits list, unstated:

The camera never tells the joke. The band can.

Every item on that list is a prop or a gesture somebody has to introduce for the camera. A banana, a gorilla suit, a pair of ape hands, a wink — none of them is a thing that would have happened anyway. The rule governs what the production does. It has never governed what happens in the room, and it cannot, because the list’s own closing test is would a joke survive being placed in this frame? A frame that could not hold five men laughing at something has already failed it.

In a teaserWhat it means
The frame stays straightNothing dressed, staged, added or pointed at for a laugh. The off-limits list applies to video unchanged.
The room is allowed to be funnyWhat the five of them actually do is the subject, not a gag. A muffed count-in, an argument about a bridge, somebody’s face after a bad take. There is no other way to get this material.
The edit reveals; it does not manufactureLet the funny thing land and cut. No zoom-in, freeze-frame, record scratch, boing, meme caption or on-screen arrow — those are the camera telling the joke, which is the gorilla suit wearing an edit suite.

The caption carries the wit, per the voice rules: the joke goes at the end, and it is not flagged. One absolute: the ape is never the joke. The band name walks into monkey comedy without any help.

This is written into both documents. A rule that lives in only one of two contradicting sources has not fixed anything — which is the same lesson the brand guide turned up on 2–3 August, when four stale cross-references were found in the sections nobody re-reads.

8

The rest of what a teaser is allowed to be

QuestionAnswer
Does the mark appear?No permanent corner bug. A mark burned into every frame is broadcast-channel behaviour — it exists so a viewer who tuned in halfway knows what they are watching. On a social clip the account name is already three centimetres above the video. An end card instead, and only when the clip has something to announce — Ink, the wordmark, the pulse rule, 1.5–2 seconds. A teaser that is just a riff and a room needs none. YouTube’s own watermark is a different thing, has a subscribe action attached, is not in the footage, and is shipped.
Red?The rule is not “take it out afterwards”, it is “do not shoot under it”. A red wash is not a colour problem, it is a texture problem, and there is no post-production answer. In a rehearsal room this costs a light switch. A red power LED on an amp is a highlight, not a field — the test is could you point at the lamp?
Vertical or landscape?Shoot once, vertically, and compose to the centre square. The destinations are vertical, a rehearsal room is a vertical subject — one person and their hands, not a stage — and a rehearsal cannot be run twice for the camera without becoming a shoot. Keeping everything important in the middle square means a 1:1, 4:5 or 16:9 crop all survive: the same move the crop proof makes for the headers, for the same reason.
Shoot in black and white?No — shoot colour, deliver black and white. Mono capture is destructive, and the first time this band has played together in eleven years cannot be restaged.
Type?Barlow, Bone only, never Steel. A real scrim behind type over footage — never dim the picture to protect a caption. The clip has to work with the type unread; if the payload is in the words it is a graphic, not a teaser. Use the platform’s own captions rather than burning them in.
How long?Verified the same way as the pixel specs. YouTube Shorts: 5 seconds to 3 minutes. X: 140 seconds and 512 MB on a free account. Instagram Reels: aspect 1.91:1 to 9:16, at least 30 fps and 720 px, with no length on the spec page. Facebook was not checked and the table says so rather than assuming it matches Instagram. X’s 140 seconds is the binding constraint across the set. Recommended, as a judgement rather than a rule: 15 to 30 seconds.
SoundThe obvious thing, said plainly because it is what sinks a teaser: the audio is the subject, and a teaser with bad sound fails whatever it looks like. A phone microphone in a loud room clips, and clipped drums cannot be repaired. The cheapest real fix is distance; the better one is a separate recording synced to the picture. Do not over-produce it either — the room noise is the character.

One tool, proposed and not installed

ffmpeg would be to video what the grading script is to photographs: a versioned recipe instead of somebody remembering settings in an app — one command emitting every per-platform crop, a stated monochrome conversion, matched loudness so one teaser is not twice as loud as the last, and durations inside each published ceiling. Free, about 100 MB, no admin needed, and clean for commercial use.

The honest counterweight is why it is not urgent: nobody cuts a teaser in ffmpeg. The edit happens on a phone. It only earns its place at the finishing step, everything above can be followed without it, and it pays for itself on the second clip. Proposed; Marcus’s call; nothing waits on it.

9

What this has not tested, and what needs Marcus

  • Not one moving frame was produced. There is no Apezone footage to test against — the archive holds 254 photographs and no video file, the two 1999–2003 promos are lost, and ffmpeg is not installed. Every rule in the video section is untested, and it is written so the 7 August rehearsal can falsify it. The three most likely to be wrong are named in the document itself.
  • The black-and-white measurement is a photographic proxy for a question about video. A still has no motion blur, no rolling shutter and no inter-frame compression.
  • Nothing has been seen on a real phone, on a real profile page, or behind a real platform’s chrome. Every image here is an exact-pixel render — which has been true of this project for seven rounds and is still true.
  • Nothing has been uploaded or posted.
  • Two SoundCloud accounts exist and which one is current is still open. The set is built once; it goes to whichever survives task 5.1.
  • The pulse rule needs its one sentence somewhere findable — one mark per year since Easter 1997. It is on six of these headers and without the caption it reads as decoration. The profile bio is the place.

Three things for Marcus, and one already answered

  1. Does the no-red rule get the scope §6 proposes? The one place the video section amends a settled document rather than applying it — and it follows directly from choosing colour. Worth settling before the 7th.
  2. The shipped circle glyphs at 16, 24 and 32 px lose ink to the mask. A real defect in a delivered file, shown in §5; the fix regenerates the whole logo set.
  3. Install ffmpeg? Proposed, not provisioned. Nothing waits on it.
  4. Answered: the avatar follows the brand guide — detailed skull at 48 px and above, round B’s below. That is what ships. §4 records the evidence that made it a question and why it was not enough to change the rule.
